The part that is not watching

The move you disagree with is the whole point.

Watching a strong game from beginning to end is pleasant and teaches less than people hope. What teaches is the moment — and there is always one — where you look at the board and think I would have taken there, and then the player you are watching does something else and wins anyway.

At that moment you can stop being a spectator. Take the position over and carry on against the engine from exactly the square where you disagreed. Play your move. Find out.

Usually you find out that your move was fine and the game was still lost, which is its own lesson: the mistake was three moves earlier and you walked past it.

How it works

  • Search by player, by event or by year.
  • Order by newest, by shortest, or by the ones you opened last.
  • Step through move by move, forwards and back, at your own pace.
  • Save a game to come back to it; the app remembers how far you got.
  • Play on from here at any position — the engine takes the other side and the coach starts grading, exactly as in a normal game.

Free and unlimited. Watch is not part of what Pro unlocks.

Where they come from

Game scores are matters of record. Annotations are not.

The scores come from published collections of the players’ own games. A record of which moves were played in a public tournament game is a fact, and facts are not copyrightable — which is why a library like this can exist at all. What is copyrightable is somebody’s commentary on those moves, so none is included: no annotations, no borrowed analysis, no exclamation marks inherited from a book.

What the app adds instead is its own — the same coach that grades your moves will grade the ones on screen if you ask it to, from the same engine, with the same win-probability scale as everywhere else. It is not a grandmaster’s opinion of a grandmaster’s move. It is an engine’s, and it says so.

Judging the players is a different mode.

Watch is for games worth seeing. Guess the Elo is for ordinary rated games where the question is not “what happened” but “how strong were these two” — sixteen hundred of them, both players within a hundred and fifty points of each other.
