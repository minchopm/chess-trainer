The short version. There is no account, no analytics, no advertising and no third-party SDK. The one thing the app fetches from us is the day’s games, when you open Today — the same files for everybody, and we keep no record of who asked. Your training ratings and history live on your device and are deleted with the app.

The one exception is rated online games. So that no device can claim a rating it did not earn, both players’ apps send our referee the game — the clock, the colours, the moves and the result — with Game Center’s proof of who is playing. It keeps your Game Center ID only as a scrambled code, with your rating on each clock; no name, no contacts. Details in section 6.

Purchases are handled by the App Store, and online play itself by Game Center. We receive no payment details from either.

3. What stays on your device

Your training ratings, solved puzzles, review schedule, streaks, game history and purchase state are stored in the app’s own container on your device. Deleting the app deletes them. They are included in an encrypted device backup if you have one turned on — that backup is between you and Apple, and we have no access to it.

There is no “export my data” button and no “delete my account” button, because there is no account and, outside online play, nothing of yours has left the device. Deleting the app is the deletion mechanism. Your online ratings are the exception: the app’s About screen has a button that deletes them from the referee (see section 6).

4. When the app uses the network

Training is entirely offline: the engine, the puzzles, the games and your progress are all in the app. The app makes network connections in exactly five situations. Three of them are connections to Apple; the others are to us:

  • Online play. Game Center finds you an opponent and carries the moves between the two devices.
  • Purchases and restores. StoreKit asks the App Store what you own.
  • Links you tap. The About screen links to the source repository, this site and the licence text. Nothing opens on its own.
  • Rated online games, and who is online. When a game that can be rated begins, and when it ends, the app tells our referee; while the app is on screen it also says, every three minutes, that you are online, unless you have turned that off (section 6). Opening the multiplayer lobby or the rank list downloads each clock’s list from brasspawn.com/media/ratings/v1/, the same files for everybody.
  • Today. Opening the Today screen downloads brasspawn.com/media/feed/v1/latest.json — the latest games from the top events — and, as you scroll back, one file for each earlier day. They are the same files for everybody, and nothing is fetched at launch or in the background. The App Clip, opened from a story’s link on this site, downloads that one story’s file the same way. The request carries no account, identifier or cookie; it reaches Amazon CloudFront, which delivers this site, with your IP address as any web request does. Request logging is off on CloudFront and on the storage behind it, so nothing is kept about who downloaded the file or when.

5. Game Center

Online play uses Apple’s Game Center to find an opponent and to carry the moves between the two devices. In a match your Game Center nickname and your in-app online rating are visible to your opponent, because that is what a game between two people needs.

During a game the two devices talk to each other and both run the rules, so a move is played only if it is legal in the position the receiving device already holds. How the game is rated is not left to either device: see section 6.

The app also puts the online rating the referee settled on, for each clock you have played, on Game Center’s leaderboards — one for each clock — and sends it again when you open the app signed in to Game Center. Other players see your Game Center nickname and when you were last seen there, as your Game Center privacy settings allow: that is how the app’s rank list shows who each player is, how its lists of active and inactive players are made, and what lets another player invite you to a game. The leaderboards are Apple’s.

Apple’s handling of Game Center data is governed by Apple’s privacy policy.

6. Online ratings: the referee

A rating that each device worked out for itself could be made to say anything, so the ratings of online games are kept by a referee of ours: a small function on Amazon Web Services, in Frankfurt, in the European Union.

What the app sends it. When a game that can be rated begins, and when it ends: your Game Center team player ID — Apple’s identifier for you in our apps, which is not your name, e-mail address or Apple Account — with Apple’s signature proving it is yours; the game’s ID; the clock; your colour; whether the game came from the open search; and, at the end, the moves and the result. Your IP address reaches Amazon, as any web request’s does. Nothing else: no nickname, no photo, no contacts, no device details. Friendly games — from an invitation, or a rematch — are not sent at all.

What it keeps. Your ID only as a scrambled code, a one-way hash of it, never the ID itself. Beside the code: your rating on each clock, how sure it is, how many rated games you have played and the day of the last one. For a day after a rated game, the two players’ codes, so the same two are rated once a day. What was sent about each game — the two reports, the moves, the verdict — is deleted after thirty days. The function’s logs, which name games by their ID and players by their code, are deleted after fourteen days.

What it publishes. Each clock’s list — codes, ratings, how sure each is, the number of games and the day of the last one — at brasspawn.com/media/ratings/v1/, which is what the app reads to show the rank list. A code says nothing about who you are; the app puts a nickname beside it only where Game Center shows that player to the person looking.

Who is online. While the app is on your screen and you are signed in to Game Center, it tells the referee that you are here — online, looking for a game on a clock, or in one — again every three minutes, and that you have gone when it leaves the screen. Other signed-in players see it beside your nickname in the list of players, so an invitation reaches somebody who is there to play. Each note lasts five minutes and is then deleted: nothing is kept about when you were online. Settings → Show when I’m online turns it off.

What it is for. Rating games and saying who is online, and nothing else. Nothing in it is sold, shared or used for advertising or tracking. The legal basis is our legitimate interest, and yours, in rank lists that cannot be forged and invitations that reach somebody who is there.

Deleting it. About → Delete my online ratings in the app removes your ratings from the referee and from every list at once. You can also write to {{ site.privacyEmail }}.

7. Purchases

The subscription and the one-off unlock are handled entirely by the App Store. We never see your name, your payment details or your Apple Account. The app only asks StoreKit whether a purchase is currently active and stores that answer locally so it can stop showing you a paywall.

Apple provides us with aggregate, anonymised sales reports — units sold per territory, and nothing that identifies a purchaser.

8. Crash reports and App Store analytics

We include no crash-reporting or analytics library. However, iOS itself has a setting — Settings → Privacy & Security → Analytics & Improvements → Share with App Developers — and if you have turned it on, Apple may share aggregated crash logs and usage statistics for this app with us through App Store Connect.

That data comes from Apple, is aggregated by Apple, and does not identify you. You control it entirely from that setting, and turning it off changes nothing about how the app works. We mention it because a policy that says “we collect nothing” while Apple hands us a crash dashboard would be, at best, incomplete.

9. The chess content

The bundled puzzles and games come from the Lichess database, released into the public domain under CC0, and from positions generated on our own machines. They contain no personal information: the imported games keep the two players’ ratings and moves, and not their usernames.

10. Children

Outside online play the app collects no personal data from anyone. The referee keeps only what section 6 describes, and nothing that identifies anybody. It shows no advertising, contains no third-party content, has no chat, no user-generated content and no external links other than to the source repository, this website and the licence text.

Online play through Game Center is subject to Apple’s own parental controls, which a parent can use to restrict multiplayer entirely (Settings → Screen Time → Content & Privacy Restrictions → Game Center).
